## Regulatory approach

The EU has adopted the most comprehensive AI-specific regulation in the world: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the Artificial Intelligence Act, in force since 1 August 2024. It is a horizontal, risk-based product-safety-style regime. Obligations scale with the risk an AI system poses: unacceptable-risk practices are prohibited, high-risk systems carry heavy provider and deployer duties, limited-risk systems carry transparency duties and minimal-risk systems, the large majority, carry none. The Act applies extraterritorially: it can reach providers and deployers outside the EU where systems are placed on the EU market or their outputs are used in the EU. Sanctions are turnover-based, up to EUR 35 million or 7 percent of global annual turnover for prohibited practices and up to EUR 15 million or 3 percent for most other breaches, which places the exposure at the level boards govern.

The AI Act sits inside a wider EU stack that AI deployments regularly trigger simultaneously: the GDPR (data protection authorities are already actively enforcing against AI processing), the Digital Services Act, the Data Act, sectoral product safety law and national implementations. Board-level compliance planning that treats the AI Act in isolation will miss most of the actual exposure.

## The timeline, as amended

The Act's staggered application was materially revised by the Digital Omnibus on AI, proposed 19 November 2025, politically agreed 7 May 2026, endorsed by the European Parliament on 16 June 2026 and given final Council approval on 29 June 2026, and **entered into force on 27 July 2026**. The deferrals below are therefore operative law, not pending changes. **Citation.** The amending act is **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744** of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 July 2026, OJ L, 2026/1744, 24.7.2026, ELI http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj, in force 27 July 2026. Its Article 1, point (40) amends the **third paragraph of Article 113** of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. **Article 113 has unnumbered paragraphs with lettered points, so the correct pinpoint form is "Article 113, third paragraph, point (c)", not "Article 113(3)".** Amended point (c) provides that Chapter III, Sections 1, 2 and 3, with the exception of Article 6(5), apply from (i) 2 December 2027 for AI systems classified as high-risk pursuant to Article 6(2) and Annex III, and (ii) 2 August 2028 for those classified pursuant to Article 6(1) and Annex I. A new point (d) provides that Articles 102 to 110 apply from 27 July 2026. Read against the consolidated text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on EUR-Lex, as at 13 August 2026 (KST).

The Omnibus also narrowed the "safety component" definition (AI that merely assists users or optimizes performance without health or safety risk is not high-risk on that ground), created a mechanism to disapply duplicative AI Act requirements where sectoral law already covers the ground, extended SME simplifications to small mid-caps (up to roughly 750 employees and EUR 150 million revenue), eased use of special category data strictly necessary for bias detection and softened the AI literacy duty from "ensure" to "take measures to support."

## What high-risk status will require

For boards planning toward December 2027, the high-risk regime is the operational heart of the Act. Providers of high-risk systems must operate a risk management system across the lifecycle, meet data governance and quality requirements, maintain technical documentation and automatic logging, ensure human oversight, meet accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity standards, complete conformity assessment, register the system in the EU database and run post-market monitoring with incident reporting. Deployers, the role most non-tech corporates occupy, must use systems per the instructions, assign competent human oversight, monitor operation, retain logs and, for employment uses, inform workers. Deployers making consequential decisions about people must be able to explain them. Readers will recognize the doctrines of this handbook translated into statute: the logging, documentation and oversight duties are [Slow AI](../handbook.md#slow-ai)'s three tests; the deployer's competent human oversight duty is [Final Liability](../handbook.md#final-liability); the intended-purpose discipline and instructions-for-use boundary is [Informed Intent](../handbook.md#informed-intent).

## Board priorities in the EU

1. **Do not read the deferral as a holiday.** Prohibitions and GPAI duties are already live; transparency duties have applied since 2 August 2026, with fines up to EUR 15 million or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; DPAs are enforcing GDPR against AI today. The penalty structure has three tiers, and boards routinely see only the first two: up to **EUR 35 million or 7 percent** for breach of the Article 5 prohibitions; up to **EUR 15 million or 3 percent** for most other operator obligations; and up to **EUR 7.5 million or 1 percent** for supplying incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to notified bodies or competent authorities. The third tier is the one that turns a botched regulatory response into its own offense, which is a reason to route authority correspondence through counsel rather than through the product team. The deferred high-risk build (conformity assessment, documentation, logging) takes well over a year for most organizations, which is exactly why the deadline moved.
2. **Classify the estate now.** Every system in the AI register should carry an AI Act classification: prohibited, high-risk (Annex III or Annex I), transparency-only or minimal. Classification drives everything and is the step organizations most often discover, too late, takes months.
3. **Chatbot and synthetic content compliance, in force since 2 August 2026.** Disclosure that users are interacting with AI, deep fake labeling and a plan for machine-readable marking by December 2026 for existing generative systems.
4. **Contract the vendor layer.** Deployers depend on providers for the documentation, logs and instructions their own duties require. Procurement should be securing AI Act cooperation clauses now, ahead of the 2027 deadline, while negotiating leverage exists.
5. **Watch the standards.** Harmonized standards from CEN-CENELEC will define practical conformity for high-risk systems; the deferral exists partly because they are late. Track them; do not wait for them to begin.

## Interaction with the other jurisdictions in this handbook

The EU model is the reference point the others react to. Korea's Framework Act and Vietnam's AI Law borrow its risk-based architecture with lighter touch; several US states borrowed and then retreated from its high-risk framing; Australia declined the model in favor of existing law. A multinational that builds to the EU high-risk standard, on the [Slow AI](../handbook.md#slow-ai) logic that governance capacity is built once and reused, will find the marginal cost of the other four jurisdictions modest.
