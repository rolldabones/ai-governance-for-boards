# Jurisdiction Guide: Australia

**Status: current as at early July 2026. Australia's National AI Plan of December 2025 confirmed that it will not introduce a standalone AI act, relying instead on existing legal frameworks, **with targeted reforms expressly left open where regulatory gaps emerge**, alongside guidance and an AI Safety Institute. Read this as a settled policy choice about method, not as a permanent commitment against AI-specific legislation: the plan itself preserves the option. Verify current status before relying on anything below.**

## Regulatory approach

Australia is the leading example in this handbook of the existing-law model. In December 2025 the Australian Government's National AI Plan confirmed that Australia will not introduce a standalone AI act, relying instead on existing legal frameworks, with targeted reforms left open where regulatory gaps emerge and a new AI Safety Institute lifting technical capability across agencies and regulators. The deliberate consequence is that the compliance burden shifts onto organizations: with no AI act to consult, each organization must map its own AI use against the full existing statute book. For boards, this model is more demanding than it sounds, because the perimeter of obligation is the whole of existing law rather than one instrument.

## Directors' duties: the sharpest edge

Board-level oversight of AI and AI-related risk forms part of directors' existing duties under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth): care and diligence, good faith, proper purpose. The securities regulator, ASIC, has been explicit that these duties extend to the adoption, deployment and use of AI, including directors' awareness of AI use within their companies, the extent of reliance on AI-generated information in discharging their duties and the reasonably foreseeable associated risks.

The boardroom-specific edge is now judicial. In ASIC v Bekier (Liability Judgment) [2026] FCA 196, the court addressed directors' use of AI to analyze board papers, observing that while obtaining such assistance is not inherently objectionable, it must not become an excuse for failing to instill discipline in the information provided to directors, nor lead to a quiet normalization of private reliance on computer-generated distillations unregulated by any agreed policy. The governance translation: board and committee AI use goes on the AI register, an agreed board AI policy governs it, directors do not substitute machine summaries for their own interrogation of board papers and director AI literacy is sufficient to understand the strengths and deficiencies of the tools, including bias, data quality, opacity and security risks. Joint professional guidance on board minutes and AI reinforces the same discipline.

## The existing-law perimeter

The obligations most commonly engaged by organizational AI use in Australia:

- **Privacy Act 1988 (Cth).** Applies wherever personal information is used to train, test or operate an AI system. From 10 December 2026, covered organizations must disclose in their privacy policies the use of computer programs in automated decision-making where decisions could significantly affect an individual's rights or interests and the program uses their personal information. A statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy has been available since 2025 and can plainly reach AI-enabled invasions. Regulator guidance on generative AI development and on commercially available AI products is in place, and tribunal decisions on facial recognition in retail have begun defining the practical boundaries.
- **Australian Consumer Law.** Misleading or deceptive conduct, unconscionable conduct, false representations, consumer guarantees and product liability all apply to AI-enabled goods and services. A 2025 Treasury review found the ACL broadly capable of adapting to AI. Enforcement is live: the competition regulator has taken action over communications to millions of customers regarding subscription changes after integrating an AI assistant into a productivity suite, has warned that AI chatbot responses must not mislead consumers and, together with ASIC, has flagged AI-washing, overstated claims about a product's AI capabilities, as an enforcement focus.
- **Anti-discrimination law.** Commonwealth and State statutes prohibit direct and indirect discrimination on protected attributes; systems trained on data reflecting historical bias can produce unlawful outcomes without any intent.
- **Work health and safety.** Harmonized WHS laws impose a primary duty of care that applies when organizations develop, procure or deploy AI, with measures including AI risks in WHS assessments, consultation and training. New South Wales amended its WHS Act in 2026 to add a duty to ensure workers are not exposed to health and safety risks arising from the allocation of work by a digital work system, a provision aimed squarely at algorithmic management.
- **Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth).** Introducing AI will often constitute a major workplace change where it significantly affects employees, triggering consultation duties and consideration of mitigation, alongside unfair dismissal, redundancy and procedural fairness protections.
- **Copyright Act 1968 (Cth).** Training or use of AI involving copyright-protected material may infringe absent consent, license or a fair dealing exception; Australia has no US-style fair use defense.
- **Cyber and critical infrastructure.** The Cyber Security Act 2024 (Cth) (minimum standards, ransomware payment reporting) and the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (Cth) impose risk management and reporting obligations relevant to AI adoption. In May 2026 the signals directorate, the prudential regulator and ASIC each issued guidance within a short period on elevated cyber risk from AI adoption, with common board messages on rapid patching and strong privileged access management.
- **Climate reporting.** Mandatory climate disclosure under the phased AASB S2 regime is likely to capture AI-related emissions indirectly through scope 2 and scope 3 disclosures, particularly data center and cloud electricity consumption.
- **Negligence.** Organizations owe a duty of care to people affected by their AI use; failing to take reasonable steps against foreseeable AI risks is actionable on ordinary principles.

## Government guidance

Australia's AI Ethics Principles (voluntary, OECD-aligned) are given operational form by the National AI Centre's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025), which consolidated the earlier ten voluntary guardrails into six essential practices: decide who is accountable; understand impacts and plan accordingly; measure and manage risks; share essential information; test and monitor; maintain human control. Two versions exist, a foundations edition and an implementation edition for governance professionals. Readers will note the six practices are a compressed statement of this handbook's operating discipline, and the first and sixth are [Final Liability](../01-doctrine/final-liability.md) and [Informed Intent](../01-doctrine/informed-intent.md) respectively in official Australian voice.

## Board priorities in Australia

1. **Treat the absence of an AI act as an assignment, not an exemption.** Map every material AI use case against the perimeter above; the mapping is the compliance program.
2. **Prepare the December 2026 privacy disclosure now.** Identifying which systems make or substantially support significant automated decisions requires the AI register to exist first.
3. **Put the boardroom's own AI use under policy.** The judicial warning was addressed to directors; an agreed board AI policy, register entries and minuted discipline are the response.
4. **Align to the six essential practices.** They are voluntary, but they are the yardstick regulators and courts will reach for when asking what a careful organization would have done.
5. **Fold AI into cyber and WHS programs explicitly.** The May 2026 tri-regulator guidance and the NSW digital work systems duty both signal where Australian enforcement attention sits.
