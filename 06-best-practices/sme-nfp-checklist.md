# SME and NFP Director Checklist

**Small and medium enterprises, non-profits and charities carry the same final liability as large corporates, with a fraction of the resources. This checklist is the proportionate version of the full handbook: what a resource-constrained board must do, and what it may legitimately defer.**

## The SME and NFP position

Smaller organizations face the AI governance problem in a distinctive form. They rarely have dedicated IT staff or data specialists, so assessing AI risks and opportunities is harder. Subscription costs are burdensome on tight budgets, and staff training loses out to immediate operational pressure. Charities and NFPs often hold data on vulnerable people, which raises the privacy and data governance stakes precisely where capacity is lowest, and resource constraints limit the ability to respond to elevated cyber risk, for example patching vulnerabilities in legacy systems quickly. Mission-driven organizations may also feel a tension between AI adoption and organizational purpose that makes them overly cautious.

None of this reduces the obligation. Regulators in every jurisdiction covered by this handbook apply directors' duties to small-organization boards, and several statutes reach small deployers directly. What the constraints change is the method: buy rather than build, use what is already licensed, take assurance in targeted slices and keep the paper trail lean but real. Limited size and resources are not an insurmountable barrier; most SME value comes from AI features inside products already in use, accounting software, productivity suites and marketing platforms, where the vendor carries most of the technical burden and the organization's job is oversight of use.

## The checklist

### AI strategy

- [ ] Understand where AI is already being used in the organization, including AI features embedded in third-party products and services. (This is the SME version of the register: one spreadsheet, honestly maintained.)
- [ ] Discuss the organization's risk appetite for AI use with management, differentiating at minimum between internal productivity use and anything touching clients, beneficiaries or their data.
- [ ] Understand whether new resources and significant investment are required to support AI deployment, or whether existing subscriptions already contain the capability.
- [ ] Assess where AI could add value and support strategic objectives cost-effectively, including enhancing existing processes and current SaaS subscriptions before buying anything new.

### AI governance structure

- [ ] Assign accountability for AI oversight to an appropriate senior leader by name; in most SMEs and NFPs this is the CEO or executive director personally. (Doctrine: Final Liability does not scale down; it just has fewer names.)
- [ ] Establish a proportionate approval process for AI systems, scaled to their potential impact on the organization and its stakeholders. A one-page approval is a real gate; an email thread is not.
- [ ] Establish when and how the board will be updated or consulted on AI systems, with simple, targeted metrics on use, effectiveness and risks.
- [ ] Consider how external assistance can support deployment cost-effectively, without becoming a substitute for internal accountability.

### AI governance practices

- [ ] Oversee a practical, accessible AI policy that sets clear boundaries for use, including restricting unapproved tools with sensitive organizational data. Public AI policy templates exist in several jurisdictions and are a legitimate starting point; the mandatory customization is naming your systems, your owner and your prohibited uses.
- [ ] Discuss the organization's specific AI risks, including the process for patching cyber and data vulnerabilities, which is where small-organization exposure is most acute.
- [ ] Ensure existing risk controls are reviewed for whether they manage AI-related risks: privacy, data governance and cyber security first.
- [ ] For NFPs holding data on vulnerable people: treat any AI touching that data as high-stakes regardless of how mundane the tool, and apply human review to outputs affecting beneficiaries.

### AI governance enablers

- [ ] Consider whether existing technology and data systems effectively support AI deployment, including whether improvements to data collection and quality are needed before, not after, adoption.
- [ ] Form a view of internal capability and invest in foundational AI training for leadership, staff, contractors and volunteers where appropriate. Free and low-cost training exists; tying tool access to its completion costs nothing.
- [ ] Engage employees, volunteers, clients and stakeholders to understand how AI use may affect them, and identify internal champions who can advocate for effective, responsible use across the organization.

## What an SME or NFP board may defer, and what it may not

Defer, with a note in the minutes: dedicated AI committees, formal role documentation beyond the named leader, full external assurance engagements, sophisticated monitoring tooling. Existing governance processes can carry AI decisions in small organizations, provided individuals understand their responsibilities.

Never defer: the named accountable human, the inventory of what is in use, the acceptable use boundary on sensitive data, human review of consequential outputs and the ability to switch off anything the organization deploys. These five are the doctrines at minimum viable scale, and they are also, not coincidentally, the elements regulators and courts examine first when a small organization's AI harms someone.
